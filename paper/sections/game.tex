\section{The Game}
\label{sec:game}

% \subsection{Concept and structure}

The artifact is a web-based, single-session educational game that supports understanding of Nielsen's ten heuristics through interactive paired scenarios. For each heuristic, the player completes the same concrete task in two interfaces: one that violates the heuristic and one that respects it. In each scenario, a narrator first announces the mission (the task the player must complete) and, after the task is completed, returns to comment on what was good or bad about the interface. Once both scenarios have been completed for a given heuristic, a reveal screen recaps the contrast side by side, explains why the compliant interface is better, and presents the heuristic with its definition. The game is bilingual (English and Portuguese) and runs entirely in the browser, with no server-side state.

The full sequence of player and narrator actions follows the same flow throughout the session:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The player enters the \emph{bad scenario} for the current heuristic.
  \item The narrator announces the mission.
  \item The player attempts the task; the bad interface produces friction, and the player works around it until the task is completed.
  \item The narrator returns to comment on what was wrong with that interface.
  \item The player enters the \emph{good scenario} for the same heuristic.
  \item The narrator again announces the same mission.
  \item The player completes the task in the compliant interface.
  \item The narrator returns to comment on what was good about that interface.
  \item The player reaches the \emph{reveal screen}, which recaps both scenarios side by side, explains why the compliant interface is better, and names the heuristic with its definition. The player is awarded a star.
  \item The player advances to the next heuristic, and steps 1--9 repeat.
  \item Once all ten heuristics have been completed, a celebratory closing screen greets the player as a ``heuristics expert'' for having collected the ten stars.
  \item A final overview screen lets the player revisit each heuristic and shows the average time spent on each. From this screen, a link leads to the post-game questionnaire described in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
  \item The player can choose to play again, restarting the cycle with progress reset to zero.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The ten heuristics}

The ten heuristics covered by the game follow the consolidated formulation by Nielsen~\cite{nielsen1994heuristic,nielsen2024tenheuristics}. Each heuristic is paired with a concrete, self-contained task that the player can complete in a few clicks on a single screen. The bad and good scenarios for the same heuristic use the same task, but the surrounding interface is intentionally redesigned so that the contrast between violating and respecting the heuristic is directly visible in the interaction. The interfaces used in the bad and good scenarios are not static mock-ups; they are interactive components that the player interacts with through real clicks, typing, and navigation. This was a purposeful design choice, so that the difference between violating and respecting a heuristic is felt through interaction rather than read as a description.

\subsection{Narration and gamification}

A narrator accompanies the player throughout the game. Before each scenario, the narrator restates the goal and primes the player's attention; after the task is completed, the narrator returns with a short commentary that names what just happened in the interface in terms of the heuristic in play. Each piece of narration is delivered both as on-screen text and as a voice-over, with the audio falling back silently to text if it cannot be played; each track also exposes pause, resume, and replay controls. The artifact does not include an in-game mute toggle; silencing the narration entirely is handled by the device or browser volume controls. The narrator is framed in the design as a focusing and reflection device, not as an authority figure who tells the player whether a choice was correct.

A few lightweight gamification elements sit on top of the educational content. A star is awarded after each reveal screen, and a small ``X out of 10'' indicator in the top bar shows the running progress. A collapsible side panel lists the ten heuristics and the player's status on each, so the player can review what has already been covered. A celebratory closing screen, reached after the tenth star, greets the player as a ``heuristics expert'' before the final results screen. These elements are intended as engagement scaffolding that helps the player stay focused and complete the full sequence in one session, rather than as a separate learning mechanism.

\subsection{Scope of the design}

A few features were deliberately left out of the artifact, and it is worth naming them explicitly. With ten heuristics to cover, each in a pair of scenarios, the design prioritized completability within a single classroom session over depth per heuristic: richer challenge mechanics such as failure states, scoring, branching paths, or longer narration would have multiplied the time per heuristic and made the total session impractical for typical class use. The game, therefore, does not adapt to the player: all participants see the same ten heuristics in the same order, and there is no adaptive difficulty. The game also does not assess learning in a quiz-style way. The bad scenarios do produce in-task friction, that is, attempts that do not advance the goal until the player works around the violation, but the player is never asked to identify the heuristic on their own, and there is no scoring of decisions. There is no collaborative or social mode; the experience is single-player and single-session, and replaying the game shows the same content. The artifact is intentionally a content-delivery and reflection-scaffolding device that complements an instructor, rather than a stand-alone teaching system or an assessment instrument.
