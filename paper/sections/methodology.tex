\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Study type and participants}

This work is an experience report on the design and qualitative evaluation of a web-based educational game that supports the understanding of Nielsen's ten heuristics. The artefact was deployed in two complementary settings: an HCI course at a Brazilian university (omitted for double-blind review) and remote participation reached through the research team's network. The classroom cohort grounds the experience in a typical teaching context, while the remote sample broadens the range of participant profiles.

Participants formed a mixed audience of undergraduate and graduate students from computing-related programs, with additional professionals and researchers reached through the broader network. Prior familiarity with Nielsen's heuristics varied across participants: some had been introduced to the heuristics by their instructor before playing, while others met them for the first time through the game. This variation is captured by a dedicated item in the demographic questionnaire, so that responses can be read against prior exposure when relevant. Recruitment was non-probabilistic; no personally identifying information was collected. The final sample comprises $N = 30$ responses; no entries were removed during data cleaning (see Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Procedure}

Each participant followed the same sequence. They opened the game in a web browser, played end-to-end through the ten heuristics in approximately fifteen minutes of interaction, and then clicked through to a linked questionnaire from the game's final results screen. The questionnaire was administered through Google Forms in Portuguese, the language approved by the ethics committee for this study. Its first page presented the informed-consent statement, which had to be accepted before continuing; the ethical care taken in this step is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:ethics}. The remaining pages contained five categorical demographic questions and five open-ended questions about the participant's experience.

The demographic questions covered current role (undergraduate, graduate student, professor or researcher, industry professional, or other), age range, primary area of work or study, prior familiarity with Nielsen's heuristics, and practical experience with HCI or usability evaluation. The open-ended questions probed: (a) overall appreciation of the game and the reason for it; (b) perceived support for understanding the heuristics, with explicit comparison to traditional reading; (c) the effect of gamification and narration on experience and learning; (d) negative points and suggested improvements; and (e) any further comments (optional). Responses were collected automatically in a linked spreadsheet accessible only to the research team.

The questionnaire deliberately omits Likert-style agreement scales. This choice reflects two design constraints. The expected sample size is small, which limits what numeric scales can reveal at this scale. The research question also targets the mechanisms behind perceived support --- something better captured by open-ended responses that participants are free to phrase in their own terms.

\subsection{Data analysis}

The open-ended responses were analysed through qualitative content analysis \cite{bardin2011}, following the four-step procedure adopted for this study. First, the data were cleaned by removing responses that were obviously not engaged with the question (for instance, single-character or filler entries), with the number of removed responses reported alongside the results. Second, the demographic answers were summarised through charts, each accompanied by a textual description so that the argument can be followed without consulting the figure. Third, the open-ended responses were grouped by question and inductively clustered into themes: similar responses were brought together and named, looking across participants for shared patterns rather than listing individual answers. Fourth, the themes were exemplified through anonymised excerpts, with participants identified only as $P_1, P_2, \ldots$; where context matters for a given quote, a short demographic tag is added (for example, ``$P_3$ (graduate student, computer science, no prior familiarity with the heuristics)'').
