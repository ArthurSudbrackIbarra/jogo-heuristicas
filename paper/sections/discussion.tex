\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The qualitative data lets us address the research question directly: in what ways does the paired-scenario game support participants' understanding of Nielsen's heuristics, and how do the surrounding design choices --- narration and light gamification --- affect that support? Four themes recur across the responses, and we discuss each in turn before stating the limitations of the study.

The first theme is that the paired bad/good contrast, combined with the interactive nature of the scenarios, is the mechanism participants most often name when explaining how the game supported their understanding (Section~\ref{sec:results:t2}). This pattern fits an experiential-learning reading of the activity: the player has a concrete encounter with a violating interface, then with a compliant one, and the reveal screen labels the abstract concept that explains the contrast. The sequence operationalises the move from concrete experience to abstract conceptualisation described by Kolb \cite{kolb1984experiential}, and the participants' own language --- $P_{30}$'s \textit{``a more active method of learning than just reading, which is a more passive way''} --- mirrors the active-learning literature \cite{bonwell1991active,freeman2014active}. The contrast itself, named by participants as \textit{``error and correct''} or \textit{``worse versus better''}, is what gives the abstract definition something concrete to attach to.

The second theme is that gamification operates as engagement scaffolding rather than as a separate learning mechanism. Participants who said gamification helped them did so by referring to focus, motivation, or the wish to finish the sequence --- not to understanding the heuristics. Several participants explicitly singled out the progress bar as helpful while reporting that the stars and the closing screen did not affect them ($P_2$, $P_5$, $P_{14}$, $P_{22}$, $P_{23}$). Two participants critiqued the gamification more structurally for not introducing a real challenge ($P_6$, $P_7$). The reading we take from this is that progress tracking carried most of the engagement value, and that the achievement layer is the most easily revised part of the design with little expected cost to the support reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:t2}.

The third theme is that the narration is useful for most participants and costly for some. About a third of the sample reported that it helped them focus or notice the heuristic in action (anchor: $P_8$). A smaller group reported the opposite effect, with complaints about its length, speech style, and the lack of an in-game way to silence it ($P_{18}$, $P_{20}$, $P_{22}$, $P_{10}$). The artefact already provides per-track pause, resume, and replay controls and an on-screen text fallback; full muting was delegated to the device or browser volume, with the expectation that players who wanted silence would use that path. The complaints suggest that this delegation was not visible to participants, and that the automatic restart of the narration at the start of each new scenario amplified the friction. Adding an in-game mute and making the per-scenario audio opt-in would resolve the consistent complaints without changing the role of the narration in the design.

The fourth theme is the recurring tension around whether the artefact is a game at all (Section~\ref{sec:results:t6}). Several participants ($P_2$, $P_7$, $P_{18}$, $P_{30}$) read it as a guided demonstration rather than as a game proper, on the grounds that there is no scored failure state and no moment where the player is asked to commit to identifying the heuristic. The bad scenarios do produce real in-task friction --- $P_8$ described being \textit{``irritated by the lack of pattern''} before the narrator named what was happening, and $P_9$ named the feeling of thinking a message had been lost --- but the player cannot be marked right or wrong about the heuristic itself, and that is the gap the critique points to. This is consistent with the design choices reported in Section~\ref{sec:game}: with ten heuristics to cover in a single classroom session, scoring, branching, and richer challenge mechanics were deliberately deferred because they would multiply the time per heuristic and make the total session impractical for class use. The notable observation in the data is that participants asked for precisely the elements that would extend the session: a recognition step before the reveal, the possibility of losing stars on mistakes, more challenging scenarios ($P_2$, $P_7$, $P_8$, $P_{18}$, $P_{25}$). This reframes the trade-off rather than dissolving it: there is room to add a recognition layer that probes the cognitive step on which novices are reported to struggle \cite{nielsen1992finding,abulfaraj2020detailed}, as long as the total session stays compatible with classroom use.

Several limitations bound the contribution of this experience report. The sample is small ($N = 31$) and self-selected; recruitment combined a classroom deployment with the research team's network, so the participants who chose to play and complete the questionnaire may have been more receptive to the activity than a random sample would have been. The sample is heavily concentrated in Computer Science and Software Engineering (25 of 31), which limits the generalisability of the findings to other disciplines. The skew reflects the deployment context (Section~\ref{sec:method}): the classroom cohort came from a computing-focused HCI course, and the network-recruited cohort largely shared this background.  The study collected no pre- or post-test of heuristic knowledge, so the claims rest on self-reported support for comprehension rather than measured learning gains. Finally, the deployment was single session, so retention is not assessed; a delayed re-evaluation would be a natural next step.
