=== REVIEWER 1 ===

Summary
This experience report presents the design and qualitative evaluation of a web-based educational artifact that supports the understanding of Nielsen's ten usability heuristics through paired interactive scenarios: for each heuristic, the player performs the same task in a violating interface and a compliant one, with a narrator framing each scenario and a reveal screen naming the concept. The artifact was evaluated through a questionnaire-based study with 31 participants, which was analyzed using qualitative content analysis. The report is well written, honestly scoped, and carefully positioned within the Brazilian HCI-teaching literature. I recommend acceptance, and the comments below are offered to help the authors strengthen the final version.  

Strengths
The paired bad/good scenario design is a genuinely good pedagogical idea, and it is cleanly motivated by the novice-anchoring problem the authors raise early (the 22/41/60% finding from Nielsen 1992 and the abstract-phrasing obstacle from Abulfaraj & Steele 2020). The related-work positioning is careful and venue-appropriate: naming Geremias et al.'s board game as the closest community artifact and distinguishing this work as web-based, digital, and single-player is exactly the right framing. Section 3.3 ("Scope of the design") is unusually candid in stating deferred features as deliberate trade-offs rather than hiding them, and Section 5.6 ("Critical reception"), which quotes participants who felt the artifact was not really a game, reflects a mature and self-aware report. Ethical care, the AI-use disclosure, and references to Global South and Brazilian community venues are all present and substantive.
  
Points for improvement (in priority order)

1. Reconsider the "game" framing. The paper consistently calls the artifact an "educational game," but by most working definitions, it is closer to scenario-based interactive instruction with a light gamification layer (stars, a progress bar, a celebratory closing screen). There is no consequential decision, no uncertainty, and no failure state. The report itself notes that the violating interface produces friction only "until the player works around it," so task completion is essentially scripted, and the stars carry no scarcity or risk. I would encourage the authors to either soften the "game" label or explicitly reposition the artifact as gamified instructional content. Relatedly, the paper currently uses "gamification," "serious games," and "game-based learning" somewhat interchangeably; briefly distinguishing these (e.g., a gamification-vs-serious-games distinction) would let the authors name precisely what they built, and would turn the participants' "this is not a game" reaction from an unanswered critique into a framing the paper owns from the start.
2. Connect the missing mechanic to the missing Kolb stage. The artifact rests theoretically on Kolb's experiential cycle, but it operationalizes only three of the four stages: concrete experience (playing the scenario), reflective observation (the narrator), and abstract conceptualization (the reveal screen). The fourth stage, active experimentation, in which the learner applies the concept to a new situation, is absent, as the player is never asked to recognize a heuristic independently. This is the same gap that participants spontaneously requested be filled (the pre-reveal recognition step, a second scenario per heuristic). I would suggest the authors make this connection explicit: the missing game mechanic and the missing Kolb stage are essentially the same gap, and closing it would simultaneously make the artifact more game-like and complete the learning cycle the paper invokes. Framed this way, the "deferred for the sake of session length" trade-off becomes a productive open tension rather than a closed decision, which would be a stronger and more interesting note for an experience report to end on.
3. Characterize the instructional context and recruitment more fully. As an experience report about teaching HCI, the paper would benefit from a clearer account of the teaching context. At present, the situating of the experience in time and space is thin: we learn the study took place in May 2026 and was "scoped to educational settings," but there is little on how participants were recruited, whether the artifact was used within an actual course or instructional session, and what the surrounding teaching setting was. A few sentences on recruitment and on the instructional context in which the artifact is intended to live would considerably strengthen the contextual grounding that this track values.
4. Add brief detail on the analysis procedure. The qualitative content analysis follows Bardin, but the procedure is described only at a high level. A short note on how many researchers coded the responses, whether themes were reviewed by more than one analyst, and how disagreements were handled would strengthen the credibility of the thematic claims. This level of rigor is not strictly required for the track, but a sentence or two would reassure the reader.
5. Minor presentation points. (a) The research question appears only in Section 6; stating it explicitly in the introduction would help the reader follow the argument from the start. (b) The participant quotes are translated from Portuguese by the authors and AI tools, which the paper transparently acknowledges; a brief note on how translation fidelity was checked would help, given that the quotes carry much of the evidentiary weight.
  
Recommendation
Weak accept. The contribution is real and useful for HCI educators and practitioners, the writing is clear, and the report's honesty about its own limits is a genuine strength. The main opportunities are conceptual (the framing of "game" and its relationship to the learning model) and contextual (the situating of the teaching experience), neither of which is fatal, and both of which the authors are well-positioned to address in a final version. 

=== REVIEWER 2 ===

This paper presents the design and evaluation of an educational support tool for supporting learners to understand Nielsen’s Usability Heuristics. The authors describe the components and educational approach of their tool, as well as results from a user evaluation with learners. 

Positives

I appreciated the detailed description of the designed tool. The authors clearly lay out the structure of the system, the user interactions, and the learning content. I found the examples used to illustrate each heuristic to be well-chosen for supporting learning. I also commend the authors’ choice to build the system around interactive screens with which the user completes tasks, supporting their ability to experience the impact of a violated heuristic rather than just reading about it. This paper makes a valuable design contribution in the form of the presented artefact and its approach to scaffolding learning around a key usability topic.

The paper offers a clear description of the methodology followed, with detailed descriptions of the participant profiles involved. The methodology is robust and appropriate for the goal of evaluating the tool for supporting learning. 

I found the findings presented to be interesting, clearly explained, and relevant to understanding how UX principles such as this might be more effectively taught. The Discussion and Final Considerations sections successfully dig into the implications of the findings for the designed tool, with well-supported recommendations and next steps outlined. These outputs would work well to inform and support any readers seeking to replicate the approach described here in terms of developing similar learning tools. 

Negatives

The ‘game’ elements of the designed artefact are limited. There are no win/ lose or scoring mechanisms built in, and the user’s progression through the system is somewhat linear. The authors address this in the paper, citing limited in-class time as the reason for these design choices, but this does raise questions for me as to whether “game” is an accurate descriptor for the developed tool. To me, the designed system is best described as an educational support tool with gamification elements – perhaps the authors could reconsider their use of language and the framing of the system in this regard. This is somewhat addressed in the Discussion when the authors reflect on the feedback from participants around their desire for scoring and failure states. However, this raises the question for me as to the authors’ initial rationale for framing this system as a game, and presumably presenting it as such to participants. Why was the system conceived of as a game, but stripped of the key elements that would allow it to function as one? Was the intention that participants would understand this as a game? Was it meant to be played or read through? If the system had been presented to participants as a content delivery system, comparable to lecture notes, would this have changed their expectations for it? Some greater clarity on this within section 3.3 would be welcome.

The image quality on the right hand side of page 5 is rather low – I would recommend replacing these with higher-resolution versions for the final paper.

I would have liked to understand how the participants were recruited. Given the heavy skewing towards computer science/ software engineering, I am interested to know if participants self-selected based on interest in learning about heuristics, or if they were recruited by the researchers. This would also provide some indication as to how relevant the topic of usability heuristics was to the users, and thus provide more context for the findings. 

I would have liked the Discussion section to broaden out beyond the scope of the designed tool to reflect on how similar approaches to scaffolding learning for UX/UI topics might be supported through interactive screen-based systems like this. For instance, graphic design/ icon usage, dark patterns, and accessibility are all topics within UI that could benefit from a similarly-design learning tool.

Overall, I found the paper to be engaging and well-structured, with clear contributions for the HCI community. While I have noted my concerns around the framing of the tool as a game, as well as some other minor issues, on balance I feel this paper should be accepted. 

=== REVIEWER 3 ===

The paper "A Web-Based Educational Game to Support the Understanding of Nielsen’s Heuristics Through Paired Scenarios: An Experience Report" presents an evaluation of a web-based educational game designed to support the teaching and learning of Nielsen’s usability heuristics. The game was evaluated by 31 participants, including undergraduate and graduate students and researchers.

As a positive aspect, the paper addresses an interesting topic that is well aligned with the track's interests. In addition, it provides an appropriate review of related work, positioning the proposed game within the existing literature on educational approaches for teaching Nielsen’s heuristics. Another positive point is that the study received approval from a Research Ethics Committee, demonstrating attention to ethical procedures in conducting the evaluation.

Regarding aspects that could be improved, the paper introduces several concepts in Section 2 that are neither sufficiently explained nor subsequently used throughout the paper, which weakens the scientific rigor of the work. Furthermore, the game's design and development are not adequately described. The paper does not explain how the game was designed, who participated in its development, which design process or methodology was followed, or how the evaluation instrument was created. Likewise, the rationale for adopting a "lightweight" gamification approach is not clearly justified, nor is the process through which this approach was incorporated into the game. Concerning the evaluation, important methodological details are missing. The paper does not describe how participants interacted with the game, how long the sessions lasted, under what conditions the study was conducted, or the overall evaluation procedure. The only information provided is that the evaluation took place in May 2026, which is insufficient to ensure the study's reproducibility.

Overall, the paper presents an interesting proposal that fits well within the scope of both the track and the IHC. The suggestions above are intended to strengthen the methodological description and improve the scientific rigor of the work, ultimately making its contributions more robust and reproducible.
